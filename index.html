The robot starts at the origin of its own odometry frame, is given a goal pose, and reports its state and pose on every control iteration.

How it works

Four states

Navigation runs as a state machine, one pass per Lidar scan at 10 Hz. The robot starts out driving straight at the goal and only stops doing that when the scan says the way is blocked.

What each state does
StateBehaviorLeaves when
DRIVE_TO_GOAL Translates straight at the goal, speed proportional to the distance left, without turning. Within 5 cm of the goal, or the path ahead is blocked.
AVOID_OBSTACLE Follows the edge of the obstacle at 0.5 m, holding its heading. Path clear on five straight scans and the robot is closer to the goal than where it met the obstacle.
TURN_TO_GOAL_ANGLE Turns in place to the goal heading. Heading error under 5°.
DONE Stops and prints the final pose. —

Holding the heading while travelling

The omni drive can translate in any direction without rotating, so the robot keeps theta fixed the whole way and turns only at the end. That keeps the Lidar slice pointed at a consistent part of the world, which makes the obstacle check much easier to reason about. The direction to the goal is rotated from the odometry frame into the body frame on every iteration, so the command stays correct however the robot is facing.

Is the path blocked?

Reading the scan

Only the part of the scan the robot is about to drive into matters. find_min_ray_in_slice() returns the shortest valid ray in a 60° slice centered on the direction to the goal. Checking a slice rather than one ray catches an obstacle sitting near the path but not exactly on it — which is the case drawn below, where the direct line to the goal misses the box but the robot would not.

That shortest ray decides the state. Two details keep the decision stable:

  • An obstacle counts only if it is nearer than the goal. Anything further away is behind the goal and must not trigger avoidance.
  • Blocking starts at 0.5 m but the path is not called clear again until 0.7 m. Without that gap between the two thresholds, a reading hovering at a single threshold flips the state on every scan.

Discussion

What we learned

Two bugs that only showed up in testing

Before taking the algorithm to the robot we ran it against a simulated Lidar and odometry, which turned up two failures that are hard to spot by reading the code.

Choosing a side from the goal direction fails when the obstacle is dead ahead. The first version picked which way to go around from the sign of a dot product with the direction to the goal. When the obstacle sits squarely in front, that dot product is zero and the choice is arbitrary: the robot took a 1.9 m detour around the far end of a wall whose near end was 0.2 m away. The fix was to sweep outward from the goal direction in 5° steps looking for the first clear 30° slice, and head for that gap instead. The near end of the obstacle is normally the first thing found, which is the short way round.

Leaving avoidance on “the path looks clear” alone livelocks. Against a U-shaped obstacle the robot flipped between driving and avoiding more than a hundred times without escaping: every time it backed away from the far wall, the way out looked clear, so it drove straight back in. Requiring the robot to also be 0.1 m closer to the goal than the point where it met the obstacle — the leave condition from the Bug 2 algorithm — makes it follow the wall around and out instead. With that one condition added, it escapes the trap and reaches the goal.

A third, smaller one: if the scan comes back with no valid rays at all while the robot is avoiding, there is nothing left to follow, and the first version sat still waiting for an edge that never came. It now returns to driving at the goal.

Tuning

Proportional control alone stalls near the goal, where the command gets too small to overcome friction, so both the translation and the rotation have a floor as well as a ceiling: the robot keeps creeping until it is inside tolerance. The ceilings keep it slow enough that one scan every 0.1 s is enough to react to something appearing in front of it.

Fill in after the demo runs
  • How close the robot actually got to each goal, and how much of that error was odometry drift rather than control error.
  • Whether LIDAR_ANGLE_SIGN needed flipping on the hardware, and how you could tell.
  • Any thresholds retuned for the real arena: obstacle distance, follow setpoint, speeds.
  • What the robot did that the simulation never showed.

Where it falls over

  • Odometry drift. Goals are in the odometry frame, which drifts as the wheels slip. A long run with a lot of edge-following ends up further from the true goal than the printed pose admits.
  • A fully enclosed goal. No bug algorithm can reach a goal that is walled off. Ours circles the enclosure rather than reporting failure; there is no give-up condition.
  • Anything below the scan plane. The Lidar sees one horizontal slice, so a low obstacle is invisible.
  • No memory of the map. Each decision uses only the current scan, so the path taken is a good guess, never the shortest one.
